Payments on account are advance payments towards your next tax bill. HMRC normally asks for two of them, each worth half of your previous year's Self Assessment bill, due on 31 January and 31 July. If your income has dropped, you can ask HMRC to reduce them, but you will pay interest if you cut them by more than you should have.

This guide explains how payments on account work, how to check the figures, and when it makes sense to reduce them. It is general information, not tax advice.

What payments on account are

When you file a self assessment tax return, HMRC works out what you owe for that tax year. If you are self-employed, a landlord or have other untaxed income, HMRC also assumes you will earn a similar amount next year and asks you to pay part of that future bill in advance. Those advance amounts are your payments on account.

Put simply, if you are asking "what are payments on account?", the answer is that they spread your tax across the year instead of leaving it all for one date. They are not an extra tax. Every pound you pay on account is credited against the bill for the year it relates to.

Payments on account in Self Assessment cover Income Tax and Class 4 National Insurance. They do not include student loan repayments or Capital Gains Tax. Those are collected in full through the balancing payment, which we cover below.

Who has to make them

Most people in Self Assessment are asked for payments on account at some point. HMRC sets them up automatically when you file your return, but there are two exemptions.

The £1,000 and 80% rules

You do not have to make payments on account if either of these applies to your last tax bill:

  • The £1,000 rule: the tax you owed through Self Assessment for the previous year was less than £1,000.
  • The 80% rule: more than 80% of the tax you owed for the previous year was collected at source, for example through PAYE on a salary or pension, or tax deducted from other income.

The 80% rule often helps people who are mainly employed with a small side business. If most of their tax is already taken through PAYE, they may only pay a single amount each January.

When they are due

There are two payment dates for each tax year:

  • First payment on account: 31 January during the tax year.
  • Second payment on account: 31 July just after the tax year ends.

For the 2026/27 tax year, which runs from 6 April 2026 to 5 April 2027, that means:

  • first payment on account: 31 January 2027
  • second payment on account: 31 July 2027
  • any balancing payment for 2026/27: 31 January 2028

How they are worked out

Each payment on account is half of your previous year's Self Assessment tax bill. HMRC uses the figure for Income Tax and Class 4 National Insurance due through Self Assessment, after taking off any tax already deducted at source.

So if your 2025/26 bill is £5,000, HMRC will expect two payments on account of £2,500 each towards 2026/27. HMRC does not look at your current trading. It assumes this year will match last year until you tell it otherwise or file the next return.

Worked example: your first year

Your first year in payments on account is usually the hardest, because you pay a full year's bill and half of the next one at the same time.

Say you started as a sole trader and your 2025/26 Self Assessment bill is £4,000. You had no payments on account for 2025/26, because your earlier bills were under £1,000. Here is what happens:

Item How it is calculated Amount Due date
Balancing payment for 2025/26 Full 2025/26 bill of £4,000 minus £0 already paid on account £4,000 31 January 2027
First payment on account for 2026/27 Half of £4,000 £2,000 31 January 2027
Total due on 31 January 2027 £4,000 plus £2,000 £6,000 31 January 2027
Second payment on account for 2026/27 Half of £4,000 £2,000 31 July 2027

In total you pay £8,000 between January and July 2027: £4,000 for 2025/26 and £4,000 in advance for 2026/27. If your 2026/27 bill turns out to be exactly £4,000, you will owe nothing more for that year in January 2028, apart from the first payment on account for 2027/28.

The balancing payment

Once you file the return for a tax year, HMRC compares your actual bill with what you paid on account. The difference is the balancing payment.

  • If your bill is higher than your payments on account, you pay the difference by 31 January after the tax year ends.
  • If your bill is lower, you have overpaid. The excess is usually set against your next payment, or you can ask HMRC for a refund.

The balancing payment also includes items that were never part of payments on account, such as student loan repayments for the self-employed and Capital Gains Tax. If you sold a second property or shares during the year, expect that tax to land in full on the January after the tax year ends.

Continuing the example, if your 2026/27 bill is £5,000, you have paid £4,000 on account, so the balancing payment due on 31 January 2028 is £1,000. On the same date the first payment on account for 2027/28 is £2,500, half of £5,000.

How to reduce payments on account

If you expect your next bill to be lower than your last one, you can reduce payments on account. You can apply if:

  • your business profits or other income have fallen
  • the tax relief you are entitled to has gone up, for example larger pension contributions
  • more of your tax will be deducted at source than last year

There are two ways to do it:

  1. Online: sign in to your HMRC online account, view your latest Self Assessment return and choose the option to reduce payments on account. You enter your estimated bill and the reason for the change.
  2. By post: fill in form SA303, print it and send it to HMRC.

HMRC sets out both routes on its page about how to claim to reduce payments on account. The deadline to claim is 31 January after the end of the tax year. If you have already paid more than the reduced amount, HMRC will refund or credit the difference.

When reducing makes sense

Reducing is worth considering when there is a clear, lasting reason your income will be lower. Common examples:

  • you lost a major client or contract part way through the year
  • you have moved from full-time self-employment to a salaried job
  • last year included one-off income that will not repeat
  • you have made a large pension contribution that increases your tax relief

The risk of reducing too far

If you reduce your payments on account and your actual bill turns out higher, HMRC charges interest on the difference between what you paid and what you should have paid. Interest runs from the original due dates of 31 January and 31 July, not from when you file the return.

A sensible approach is to reduce only by an amount you are confident about, base it on your bookkeeping to date, keep a note of how you reached the estimate, and review it again before the 31 July payment.

Paying in instalments and budgeting

Payments on account are easier to manage if you treat tax as a regular cost rather than a twice-yearly shock. A few practical habits help:

  • Set aside a fixed percentage of every payment you receive into a separate savings pot. Many sole traders use somewhere between 20% and 30%, depending on their profit level.
  • Pay early if it suits you. You do not have to wait for the due date to pay towards your bill.
  • Diary both dates. July catches people out more than January, because there is no return to file and no reminder from the filing deadline.
  • Check your statement in your HMRC online account so you know the exact figure before each payment date.

Payments on account under MTD

Making Tax Digital for Income Tax became mandatory from 6 April 2026 for sole traders and landlords with qualifying income over £50,000. If you are in it, you send quarterly updates of your income and expenses through compatible software.

MTD does not change the payment dates. Quarterly updates are not tax returns and no payment is due when you send them. Payments on account are still due on 31 January and 31 July, and the balancing payment is still due on 31 January after the tax year ends.

What MTD does give you is a clearer view of your figures during the year, which makes it easier to spot early whether reducing payments on account is justified, or whether you should put more aside.

FAQ

Do I have to make payments on account in my first year of self-employment?

Not usually for the first year itself. Payments on account are based on your previous bill, so they start after you file your first return, provided that bill was £1,000 or more and less than 80% of your tax was collected at source.

Can I stop payments on account completely?

You can reduce them to zero if you expect your next bill to be nil, or if you expect to fall within the £1,000 or 80% rules. If your bill turns out higher, you will pay interest on what you should have paid.

What happens if I overpay on account?

If your actual bill is lower than your payments on account, the excess is normally set against your next payment. You can also ask HMRC to repay it through your online account.

Is Capital Gains Tax included in payments on account?

No. Capital Gains Tax and student loan repayments are left out of payments on account. They are paid in full as part of the balancing payment on 31 January after the tax year ends. You can read more on GOV.UK's guide to payments on account.
