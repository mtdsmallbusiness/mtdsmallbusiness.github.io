Digital record keeping for MTD means recording every business income and expense transaction in software or a spreadsheet, with the date, the amount and a category for each one. You can use accounting software with a bank feed, or a spreadsheet plus bridging software, as long as the data moves between products through digital links rather than retyping. Paper receipts do not have to be scanned, but you still need to keep the originals or copies as supporting evidence.

This guide covers what HMRC expects under Making Tax Digital for Income Tax in the 2026/27 tax year, the main ways to keep records, and a simple monthly routine. It is general information, not tax advice, so check your own situation with HMRC or an accountant.

What counts as a digital record under MTD

A digital record is an entry for a single business transaction, held in software that can store it and pass it on. Under Making Tax Digital digital records rules, this applies to your self-employment and UK or foreign property income and expenses.

The digital record is not the receipt itself. It is the line in your software or spreadsheet that describes the transaction. The receipt, invoice or bank statement behind it is supporting evidence, which you keep alongside the record.

What each record must show

According to HMRC's guidance on creating digital records, each record needs three things:

  • The amount of the income received or the expense paid.
  • The date the income was received or the expense was incurred.
  • The category the transaction belongs to.

MTD for Income Tax uses the same income and expense categories as Self Assessment, such as office costs, travel, staff costs or property repairs. If your turnover is below £90,000, you can choose simpler categorisation for a tax year, which for a sole trader means only recording whether each item is income or an expense. Landlords with UK residential property still need more detail on some expenses, including whether a cost is a restricted finance cost.

Digital links

If you use more than one software product, HMRC requires them to be digitally linked. That means data moves from one product to another electronically, not by hand. HMRC's page on keeping digital records lists accepted methods, including:

  • linked cells within or between spreadsheets
  • emailing a spreadsheet containing digital records
  • moving files on a portable device such as a memory stick
  • importing and exporting XML or CSV files
  • automated data transfer or transfer through an API

What is not allowed is manually moving a record once it is in your system. Retyping a figure into another cell or another product, or using copy and paste to move records, breaks the digital link. If you spot an error, correct it in your record keeping software as soon as you can so the fix flows through to your next quarterly update.

Ways to keep digital records

There is no single required setup. HMRC's guidance on choosing the right software describes software that creates records by importing bank transactions, scanning receipts or taking manual entries, and bridging software that connects to records you already keep. Most small businesses end up with one of the three approaches below, or a mix.

Accounting software with bank feeds

Full accounting software keeps your records and sends quarterly updates and your tax return from one place. The most useful feature for most people is a bank feed: a connection to your business account that pulls transactions in automatically every day.

With a feed, the date and amount arrive without any typing. Your job is to assign a category and attach evidence. Many products let you set rules, so a recurring phone bill or software subscription is categorised the same way each month. This approach suits people with lots of transactions, card payments, or a mix of income sources.

Spreadsheets and bridging software

If you already keep good spreadsheets, you can carry on. An MTD spreadsheet is acceptable as a digital record, provided it holds the date, amount and category for each transaction. What a spreadsheet cannot do is talk to HMRC, so you need MTD bridging software to read the figures and submit them.

The key point is the link between the two. The bridging software should pull totals directly from the spreadsheet, for example by reading the file or specific cells. Summarising your figures on a separate sheet with formulas is fine. Typing the totals into the bridging software by hand is not.

This route suits people with fewer transactions who are comfortable with formulas and want to keep control of their layout. The trade off is more manual entry and more room for typing errors, since there is usually no bank feed.

Receipt capture apps

Receipt capture apps let you photograph a paper receipt, read the date, amount and supplier from the image, and file it. Some are standalone, while others are built into accounting software.

On their own, they rarely cover everything you need, because income usually does not arrive as a receipt. They work best as a front end that sends data into your main records through an import, an export file or an API. That keeps the digital link intact and saves you hunting for paper at the end of the quarter.

Keeping business and personal money apart

MTD does not force you to open a separate business account, but mixing personal and business spending makes digital record keeping much harder. Every personal transaction in your feed or spreadsheet is one more line to identify and exclude, and one more chance to claim something that is not allowable.

A dedicated account for business income and expenses gives you a cleaner bank feed and a clearer audit trail. If you do pay for something business related from a personal account, record it in your business records with the receipt attached, and treat it consistently. For costs with mixed use, such as a phone or vehicle, record the business proportion only and keep a note of how you worked it out.

A simple test: if HMRC asked about any line in your records, could you show the receipt or invoice and explain why it is a business cost?

A monthly record keeping routine

Quarterly updates are easier when the work is spread across the year. A short monthly session keeps records accurate and avoids a rush before each deadline.

  1. Bring in transactions. Let your bank feed refresh, or import the month's statement into your spreadsheet using a CSV file rather than typing it out.
  2. Categorise everything. Assign each transaction to the right Self Assessment category, or to income or expense if you use simpler categorisation.
  3. Match evidence. Attach or file receipts and invoices against each expense, and chase anything missing while you still remember it.
  4. Reconcile with the bank. Do a quick bank reconciliation so the closing balance in your records matches your statement and nothing is missing or counted twice.
  5. Check cash and other accounts. Record cash takings, cash expenses and any payments from cards or accounts that are not linked.
  6. Fix errors. Correct mistakes in the original record so the change carries through your digital links.
  7. Back up. Make sure spreadsheets and exported files are saved somewhere secure and not only on one laptop.

At the end of each quarter, review the totals before your software or bridging software sends the update. Because the monthly work is done, this should take minutes rather than an evening.

How long to keep records

HMRC says you must keep your digital records for at least 5 years after the 31 January submission deadline for the relevant tax year. For the 2026/27 tax year, the tax return deadline is 31 January 2028, so records for that year should be kept until at least 31 January 2033.

The same applies to supporting documents such as receipts, invoices and bank statements. You can keep them on paper, or keep copies, as long as they stay readable for the whole period. If you change software, export your data before cancelling a subscription so you do not lose access to past years.

Common mistakes

  • Retyping totals into bridging software. This breaks the digital link. Let the bridging software read the spreadsheet directly.
  • Copying and pasting between products. HMRC lists copy and paste as a manual transfer. Use an import, export or linked cells instead.
  • Leaving categorisation until the deadline. Uncategorised bank feed lines pile up quickly and lead to guesswork.
  • Treating the receipt app as the full record. Income and transactions without a receipt still need to be recorded.
  • Throwing away paper too early. Scanning is optional, and the original or a readable copy must be kept for the full retention period.
  • Mixing personal spending into business records. It inflates expenses and makes reconciliation slow.
  • Forgetting cash. Cash income and expenses do not appear in a bank feed and are easy to miss.

FAQ

Can I use a spreadsheet for MTD?

Yes. A spreadsheet can hold your digital records if it shows the date, amount and category of each transaction. You then need bridging software that is digitally linked to the spreadsheet to send quarterly updates and your tax return to HMRC.

What is MTD bridging software?

Bridging software connects to records you already keep, such as spreadsheets, and sends the figures to HMRC. It does not usually replace your records. It reads them, so the link between the spreadsheet and the bridging software must be digital.

Do I have to scan every receipt?

No. HMRC's MTD guidance does not require receipts to be scanned. Your digital records must be in software or a spreadsheet, but supporting documents can be kept as originals or copies. Scanning is simply a convenient way to keep copies safe and searchable.

Do I need a bank feed?

No. A bank feed is a convenience, not a requirement. You can import statements as CSV files or enter transactions manually into your record keeping software, as long as you do not later move records between products by hand.

What happens if I find a mistake after sending a quarterly update?

Correct the record in your software as soon as possible. Changes made during the tax year are picked up in your next quarterly update, and all corrections need to be made before you finalise your tax position for the year.
